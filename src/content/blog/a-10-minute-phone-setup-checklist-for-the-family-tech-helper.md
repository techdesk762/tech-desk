If you are the person in your family who gets the "can you look at my phone?" call, new phones are your busy season. Upgrades are likely soon, and one setup choice you make on day one can shape what your relative's phone does for years.

Mozilla, the maker of Firefox, wrote an open letter to people in that role. Its main request is narrow: help your relatives set Firefox as their default browser. This article covers what that means, how to do it, and where the letter stops.

## What this means for you

A browser is the app that opens websites. On most phones, a default browser comes preinstalled. It stays in charge unless someone deliberately changes it.

That matters because of what a browser can see. Mozilla points out that a browser can see every site you visit, every phrase you search and every link you tap, which makes it one of the biggest channels for personal data leaving a phone.

If you set up phones for relatives, you probably make that choice for them without thinking about it. People in some regions, such as Europe and Japan, are offered a browser choice when they set up a phone. Mozilla says that is not the case everywhere, so the helper's choice often becomes the final choice.

One catch: installing Firefox is not enough. Links in messages, email and other apps keep opening in the preinstalled browser until the default changes.

## What changed

On November 12, 2026, Mozilla published an open letter to the informal family IT person. It asks them to install Firefox and set it as the default when setting up a new phone.

The timing is tied to new Android and iPhone releases and the approach of Black Friday deals, which should send many people to upgrade.

Mozilla's pitch includes a few claims:
- Firefox is built so that even Mozilla should not know which sites you visit.
- Mozilla says it is backed by a nonprofit, with no shareholders.
- Enhanced Tracking Protection, which blocks known trackers (hidden bits of code that follow you around the web), is on by default on Android and iOS.
- An AI controls section lets people customize AI features or turn them off.

## What to do today

Mozilla suggests about five extra minutes on day one. Do this with your relative's phone in hand.

1. **Install the Firefox app** on the new phone from the usual app store.
2. **On Android, set it as default.** Open Firefox, tap the three-dot menu, then Settings. Under General, choose Set default browser. Turn on the toggle, pick Firefox from the list, then tap Set as default.
3. **On iPhone, set it as default.** Go to Settings, then Apps, then Default Apps, then Browser App. Choose Firefox.
4. **Test it.** Tap a link in a text message or email. Mozilla says links from messages, email, social media and other apps should now open in Firefox automatically.
5. **Look at the privacy settings together.** Enhanced Tracking Protection is on by default. You can switch it off or choose Strict mode for stronger protection, and the AI controls section lets you adjust or turn off AI features. The letter does not give menu paths for these settings, but Mozilla links to help pages: [Android](https://support.mozilla.org/kb/enhanced-tracking-protection-firefox-android), [iPhone](https://support.mozilla.org/kb/enhanced-tracking-protection-firefox-ios) and [AI controls](https://blog.mozilla.org/firefox/how-to-use-ai-controls/).
6. **Tell your relative what you changed.** A quick sentence helps, such as "Links now open in Firefox." It avoids a confused phone call a week later.

## Our take

Our take: the useful idea here is not really about Firefox. It's that first-day setup choices tend to stick. Mozilla's letter makes the same point, urging about five extra minutes on day one. Whatever browser you prefer, setting up a relative's phone deliberately beats leaving everything on factory settings.

We'd also be careful with the framing. Mozilla makes Firefox and wants more people to use it, so this is a pitch from an interested party. The claims about its design, its nonprofit backing and what Mozilla can or can't see are Mozilla's own statements. We haven't tested them, and you may want to read the company's privacy information before deciding. Switching browsers is a reasonable privacy step, but it is only one step.

The letter also doesn't cover other things a family helper might care about, such as software updates, password managers or spotting scam messages. Those are common sense additions to any setup visit, and we'd suggest them, but they are our suggestion rather than part of Mozilla's letter. Our recommendation: pick the browser with your relative, set it as default, run the link test, and then spend any remaining time on those basics. Keep it simple enough that they can remember it without you.

## What we don't know yet

- **Where the privacy and AI settings sit.** The letter doesn't give menu paths for these settings; Mozilla's help pages, linked above, do.
- **How much it changes day-to-day browsing.** Mozilla says tracking protection works with no setup. We don't know how noticeable that is for an ordinary user, or whether Strict mode breaks any sites.
- **Whether the steps match every phone.** The Android and iPhone steps come from Mozilla's letter. Menu names can differ between phone makers and software versions, so you may need to look around a little.
- **Whether a choice screen is coming to more regions.** Some places already offer one at setup. The letter doesn't say that will spread.
- **How well the claims hold up.** Mozilla says even it should not know what sites users visit. We have no independent verification, and the letter doesn't offer details we can check here.

For the exact wording and Mozilla's full argument, read the original [open letter](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/set-firefox-default-mobile-browser/).
