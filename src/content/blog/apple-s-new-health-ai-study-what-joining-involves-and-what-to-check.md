If you have ever typed a health question into a chatbot, Apple now wants to study how that goes. It has opened a research study in the US, and you can take part if you meet a few requirements.

Joining is optional, and so is sharing your personal health data. Before you enroll, it helps to know what you would be asked to do and what you can decide for yourself.

## What this means for you

The study is called the Apple Health AI Usage Study. It runs in its own companion app. Participants ask everyday health questions to an AI tool and get answers in plain language.

Apple is running it with OpenEvidence. This is a clinical AI platform that Apple says is widely used by healthcare professionals. Its answers draw on medical sources including NEJM, JAMA, and Nature. Each answer comes with links to peer-reviewed clinical citations, so you can check where the information came from.

You can also choose to share some health data from your iPhone. This goes through HealthKit, which is the system iPhone uses to store health information. Examples Apple gives are resting heart rate, activity, sleep, or lab results. The idea is to see whether personal data makes answers more relevant and easier to understand.

The study will also look at whether AI can help people get ready for visits with their own doctors.

Two points are worth knowing up front:

- Apple says study data will not be used to train AI models.
- The app does not diagnose or treat conditions, and it does not replace medical advice.

## What changed

On November 15, 2026, Apple [announced the study](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/11/apple-launches-research-study-to-explore-the-use-of-ai-for-health/). It looks at how people use conversational health AI tools. That includes what builds trust, how good the answers are, whether people understand them, and what they do afterward.

Apple says findings are meant to guide responsible design of health AI, for itself and the wider industry. It also says AI health tools can sometimes give incomplete or unclear answers, which is why it wants the research.

Up to 200,000 eligible US participants can join.

## What to do today

1. **Check the basics.** You must be in the US and at least 21 years old.
2. **Check your phone.** You need an iPhone with Apple Intelligence turned on. Apple lists the iPhone 15 Pro or later, running iOS 26 or later.
3. **Download the study app to learn more.** Apple says you can do this before choosing whether to enroll.
4. **Read before you agree.** Look at what the app says about the data it collects and how it is handled.
5. **Decide what to share.** Sharing HealthKit data and survey responses is your choice. Sharing nothing is an option.
6. **Find the withdrawal controls.** Look for them inside the study app. Our notes from Apple's announcement do not say where in iPhone settings you can review or withdraw permissions. We won't guess at a menu path. Check the app itself and Apple's announcement.
7. **Keep a doctor in the loop.** If a question is about a real health worry, Apple says to consult a clinician.

## Our take

What matters most here is the research question, not the chatbot. Many people already ask AI about symptoms and test results. Little is known about whether those answers are understood, trusted, or acted on. A study that looks at all of that is useful, especially if its findings shape how these tools are built.

The citations are a good feature. Links to sources let you check an answer rather than take it on faith. That said, a linked source does not guarantee that the answer reads it correctly. Apple itself says such tools can give incomplete or unclear answers. Treat the app as a way to learn and prepare questions, not as a verdict.

Our recommendation: if you are curious, download the app and read its terms first. If you enroll, start with the least sharing you are comfortable with. You can answer questions without sharing HealthKit data. Add the personal data only if you see a clear reason to, and only after you know how to withdraw.

## What we don't know yet

- **Where to manage permissions.** The announcement notes we have do not name the exact iPhone settings location for reviewing or withdrawing data-sharing. The app or Apple's page may explain it.
- **How long the study runs.** We have no end date or time commitment.
- **Any payment.** Nothing in our notes mentions compensation.
- **How data is protected.** Apple says it has security measures for the data it maintains. It gives no details in our notes.
- **Which concerning questions trigger the safety checks.** The app has guardrails that detect certain concerning questions and suggest following up with a provider or other resources. We don't know exactly what they catch.
- **When the results will be shared.** Apple says the findings are meant to guide design. We don't know if or when they will be published.
- **Whether it will expand beyond the US.** The study launched in the United States only.

We haven't tested the app. This article is based on Apple's announcement.
