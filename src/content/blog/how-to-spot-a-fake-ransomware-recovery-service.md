If ransomware locks your files, you will be scared, rushed and looking for someone to call. That is exactly when a bad "recovery" service can do the most damage.

A new U.S. indictment shows how that can allegedly play out. It is only an allegation so far, and nothing has been proven in court. But the warning signs it describes are useful to know before you ever need them.

## What this means for you

Ransomware is software that locks your files and demands money to unlock them. If it hits you or your small business, you may look for outside help. Some of that help may not be what it claims.

According to prosecutors, one firm promised never to deal with the attackers. It allegedly did exactly that, quietly, and billed victims far more than it paid. Victims who thought they were buying clever technical work were allegedly buying a hidden middleman.

You do not need to be technical to protect yourself. You need to know which claims and behaviors should make you stop and ask questions.

## What changed

Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, 52, of Miami and Medellin, Colombia, was indicted in Rhode Island on wire fraud charges. He co-founded RansomwareHelp, a Florida business about six years old whose website is now defunct. It advertised tools for restoring data locked by ransomware.

Its website claimed a success rate above 99%, 28 years of experience and 80 experts. It also promised never to pay or intermediate ransoms. Prosecutors say it did pay ransomware groups for decryption keys (the digital "keys" that unlock files) and billed victims much more.

[Krebs on Security reported the details](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/11/cto-of-ransomwarehelp-indicted-for-wire-fraud/), including an FBI sting and a 2024 case.

## What to do today

You can do these in about 10 minutes, before any emergency.

1. **Write down your plan.** Note who you would call first if files were locked. Decide now that you will not hire the first search result under pressure.
2. **Learn the red flags.** Be wary of near-perfect success rates, huge experience claims, and a loud promise that they "never" deal with criminals. The firm's own website made claims like these, according to Krebs on Security.
3. **Plan to ask direct questions.** Ask in writing: "If you talk to the attackers, will you show me every message and tell me exactly what they asked for?" Get the answer before you sign anything. Hiding who is talking to the attackers is a warning sign.
4. **Plan to refuse blanket remote access.** In the 2024 case, the firm demanded unrestricted remote access through AnyDesk, a remote-control program. A security researcher believes it was used to delete ransom notes. Don't hand over unlimited control without understanding why it's needed.
5. **Plan to get a second quote.** If a price seems enormous or the technical explanation is unclear, ask another firm. In the 2024 case, the victim hired a different firm and paid about half the first quote.
6. **Keep the ransom note.** If you are ever hit, make sure a copy is saved and don't let anyone delete it. Paul Ihme, quoted by Krebs, says a legitimate firm would simply ask you for a copy of the note, and he sees deleting copies as a red flag.

## Our take

What matters most here is the hidden middleman. Prosecutors say the firm sold itself as a technical rescuer, then acted as a secret ransom broker with a large markup. In the FBI sting, the fake victim was allegedly told decryption would cost $13,260, while the firm negotiated the gang's price down to about $6,000. The victim's total bill was $16,010, including a $2,750 diagnostic fee.

The 2024 case shows how pressure works. The proposal reportedly came to $438,880 and cited 510 forensic servers and irrelevant technical terms like SHA-256. Paul Ihme, co-founder of the security firm Soteria, told Krebs that many of the technical claims were farcical. A confident wall of jargon is not proof of skill. In our view, a trustworthy provider can explain what it will do in plain words and put its fees in writing.

A caution: this is one indictment, and the cases mentioned alongside it are separate. One executive was charged with cyber extortion (case sealed). Another was charged with wire fraud over an alleged $8 million paid versus $19 million billed. A negotiator got 70 months in prison for leaking a client's strategy to a ransomware group. We would not conclude the whole industry is crooked. But we'd treat "recovery" services as something to vet carefully, not trust on a promise.

Our recommendation: the best defense is to need these services less. Keep backups (spare copies of your files) stored somewhere the infected computer can't reach, and test that you can restore from them. The source article doesn't cover backups, so that advice is ours. If you do get hit, call a firm you vetted in advance, not one that found you.

## What we don't know yet

- **The outcome.** An indictment is a charge, not a verdict. The source article does not include a response from Palacio.
- **How many victims.** We don't know how many people or businesses were charged more than the real cost.
- **The other cases.** The Cybersteward case is sealed, so details are limited.
- **Where to report.** The source article does not name a reporting site or agency for people who think they were overcharged. Check the website of your country's cybercrime authority for current instructions.
- **How to vet a firm.** The source article does not say what a legitimate provider's credentials look like. Treat the warning signs above as a starting point, not a full checklist.
